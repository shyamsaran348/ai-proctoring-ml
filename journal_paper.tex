%                         ABSTRACT

\begin{abstract}
The maintenance of examination integrity in remote educational environments necessitates advanced automated proctoring systems. Current methodologies often rely on instantaneous, rule-based detection, which typically results in excessive false alerts and fails to account for the temporal progression of behavioral patterns. This study reformulates the problem of integrity monitoring through a multi-signal temporal behavioral inference framework. The proposed architecture integrates seven neural components, including six specialized temporal models that analyze identity stability, presence, attentiveness, long-horizon drift, and head-gaze dynamics. These components are anchored by a one-shot identity embedding engine that operates under a strictly immutable enrollment constraint to prevent unauthorized identity adaptation. A Gated Recurrent Unit is employed as a central fusion engine to synthesize continuous behavioral signals into a comprehensive session-level risk trajectory. A significant feature of the developed system is its uncertainty-aware formulation, which models risk through a probabilistic Gaussian distribution. This approach provides both a continuous risk estimate and an explicit metric of prediction confidence, allowing for a principled distinction between clear integrity violations and ambiguous sensor noise. Empirical evaluation involving five thousand procedurally generated sessions across diverse behavioral categories indicates that temporal fusion achieves superior session-level performance compared to individual component analyses. The integration of continuous uncertainty estimation fundamentally enhances the system’s ability to avoid unjustified accusations during noisy recording conditions. Computational profiling demonstrates that the framework is feasible for real-time application on standard processing units. These findings suggest that the accumulation of temporal evidence, coupled with probabilistic uncertainty modeling, provides a more robust foundation for high-stakes behavioral monitoring than traditional threshold-based event detection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} expansion of digital learning platforms has intensified the requirement for reliable, automated monitoring of examination integrity. Remote assessments are vulnerable to various forms of academic misconduct, ranging from direct impersonation to more subtle patterns such as gradual identity substitution or intermittent gaze aversion that can bypass basic detection mechanisms. Two primary paradigms currently dominate the field, each possessing inherent structural limitations.

Heuristic, rule-based systems typically utilize predefined thresholds for specific behavioral markers, such as gaze orientation or head pose. These thresholds, often established in controlled environments, frequently lack robustness when faced with the inconsistent lighting and varied camera angles found in home settings~\cite{Nigam2020, Atoum2017}. Furthermore, intentional cheating strategies that carefully maintain signals within acceptable limits while exhibiting anomalous combinations over time often remain undetected by traditional threshold-based systems.

Alternatively, static binary classifiers treat integrity monitoring as a series of isolated decisions per frame. While deep learning models offer improved robustness compared to manual feature engineering~\cite{Doughty2022, Ghizlane2019}, they often neglect the essential temporal context that helps differentiate regular, transient behaviors from sustained anomalous activity. Although contemporary research explores architectures like Vision Transformers for spatial-temporal analysis, these models can be computationally intensive for real-time edge processing and may not always effectively isolate different behavioral modalities.

The fundamental premise of this research is that proctoring should be viewed as a continuous process of temporal inference. Individual signals are frequently noisy at the frame level; for instance, a temporary decrease in identity similarity might simply result from a shift in illumination. However, when multiple signals display coordinated anomalous behavior over a sustained period, the indication of an integrity breach becomes statistically significant. This principle of sequential evidence gathering is well-recognized in statistical hypothesis testing and anomaly detection but has not been comprehensively applied to the domain of online exam proctoring~\cite{Wald1945, Liu2018, Chandola2009}.

The proposed study introduces the Temporal Behavioral Inference Engine (TBIE), a neural architecture designed to implement this continuous inference approach. The system extracts several continuous behavioral signals from standard video feeds, analyzes each through specialized temporal models, and integrates the findings via a recurrent fusion engine that maintains a persistent state of session evidence. Instead of relying on instantaneous frame-level classification, the framework generates a continuous risk trajectory accompanied by a dynamic measure of uncertainty.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} The primary contributions of this work include: the development of a multi-signal temporal fusion architecture that adheres to specific design invariants; scientific validation demonstrating that temporal evidence accumulation significantly outperforms individual signal analysis; a comprehensive performance study involving ablation, baseline comparisons, and calibration; the implementation of a gradient-based attribution mechanism for frame-level justification; the introduction of a specialized model for head-pose and gaze decorrelation; and an uncertainty-aware probabilistic framework that distinguishes behavioral anomalies from sensor noise. This work reframes proctoring as a temporal probabilistic inference problem over weak behavioral signals.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Hardware-Based and Biometric Approaches}
Early proctoring research employed specialized hardware---biometric mice for keystroke dynamics~\cite{Traore2011} and multi-camera setups to eliminate webcam blind spots~\cite{Atoum2017}. While these systems offered high detection fidelity, their deployment cost drove the field toward software-only solutions using commodity webcams.

\subsection{Static Computer Vision Methods}
Li~et~al.~\cite{Li2018} used iris detection via Hough transforms and geometric rules for gaze estimation, proving brittle under varied lighting. Deep CNN approaches improved per-frame robustness~\cite{Doughty2022, Ghizlane2019} but ignore the temporal context distinguishing momentary glances from sustained suspicious behavior.

\subsection{Identity Verification}
Developments in metric learning, such as FaceNet~\cite{Schroff2015}, ArcFace~\cite{Deng2019}, and CosFace~\cite{Wang2018}, have established robust foundations for one-to-one identity matching. However, many commercial implementations rely on adaptive template updates, which introduces an identity drift vulnerability. The proposed framework explicitly addresses this by employing an immutable enrollment anchor.

\subsection{Temporal and Sequential Methods}
Research into temporal modeling for anomaly detection has spanned various domains, including video surveillance~\cite{Liu2018, Sultani2018} and network analysis~\cite{Mahoney2003}. Early attempts at behavioral analysis frequently utilized late fusion strategies, where independent flags from fixed-window heuristics were aggregated through majority voting. In contrast, contemporary sequential anomaly detection~\cite{Chandola2009} favors early or continuous fusion, enabling models to maintain long-term memory that captures behavioral evolution. While affective computing and engagement analysis~\cite{Dewan2019, Garcia2021} offer related insights, the current architecture extends the continuous fusion paradigm by operating on multiple heterogeneous signals through specialized models, delaying final classification until the comprehensive session risk trajectory is formed.

\subsection{Explainability in High-Stakes AI}
The deployment of AI in educational settings necessitates high levels of interpretability~\cite{Arrieta2020}. Given that neural networks can often be miscalibrated~\cite{Guo2017}, post-hoc attribution methods~\cite{Sundararajan2017} are vital for validating model decisions. The developed temporal attribution mechanism satisfies these requirements by generating frame-level signal importance maps alongside calibrated probability estimates.

\section{Dataset and Data Provenance}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Dataset Description}

The evaluation of the Temporal Behavioral Inference Engine (TBIE) is conducted using a hybrid multi-source dataset specifically engineered to capture the nuances of high-stakes proctoring environments. The dataset comprises 5,000 unique exam sessions, each containing 120 frames recorded at a target inference rate of 30\,FPS (${\approx}4$\,s per session). These sessions are partitioned into various behavioral categories, including genuine participation and several classes of integrity violations such as impersonation, identity drift, and gaze-based anomalies.

To ensure an authentic identity foundation for proctoring, the identity-related components (\textbf{IDE} and \textbf{LDD}) utilize the \textbf{VGGFace2} dataset~\cite{Cao2018}. VGGFace2 is a large-scale face imagery collection containing approximately 3.31 million images across 9,131 unique identities, exhibiting significant variations in pose, age, and illumination. For this study, the images were pre-processed using a Multi-task Cascaded Convolutional Network (MTCNN) to extract facial bounding boxes and align facial landmarks to a standardized $160{\times}160$ resolution. A ResNet-50 backbone, initially pre-trained on ImageNet, was subsequently fine-tuned using a triplet loss objective to maximize the inter-session discriminability across thousands of unique individuals.

For the \textbf{Presence and Attentiveness Model (PAM)}, the synthetic baseline is augmented by a dedicated \textbf{Recorded Video Dataset (RVD)}. The RVD consists of proprietary MP4 sessions captured at 720p resolution across a diverse cohort of volunteers. These sessions capture authentic face-camera entrance and exit events, as well as natural head movement patterns. The RVD reflects heterogeneous indoor environments with variable illumination intensities (ranging from 150 to 600 lux) and realistic background clutter, providing a robust validation set for presence detection under authentic conditions.

The remaining behavioral signals---Identity Instability ($U$), Gaze ($G$), and Head-Gaze Dynamics ($H$)---are generated through a sophisticated procedural signal modeling process. Each session $j$ is initialized with a mean behavioral state $\mu_j$ and evolved through a first-order autoregressive AR(1) process to ensure temporal smoothness:
\begin{equation}
    x_t = \phi\, x_{t-1} + (1{-}\phi)\,\mu + \epsilon_t, \quad \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)
    \label{eq:ar1}
\end{equation}
where $\phi{=}0.85$ is the temporal autocorrelation factor selected to empirically mirror the smooth but stochastic movement patterns observed in actual webcam feeds. Specifically, the \textbf{Synthetic Gaze} signals ($G_t$) are modeled to reflect realistic behavioral signatures. Genuine attentive behavior is simulated as stable AR(1) fluctuations around the screen center. Conversely, \textbf{scanning behavior} is modeled as a cyclic sideways search pattern using oscillating yaw components with a period of approximately 2.5\,s, while \textbf{phone-usage signatures} are introduced as sustained downward pitch deflections ($\mu_{\text{pitch}} < -20^\circ$) with increased variance.

To further approximate real-world environmental conditions, the generative pipeline incorporates three specific stochastic perturbation types:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Noise Bursts}: Modeling transient lighting shifts and sensor gain fluctuations through additive Gaussian noise $\epsilon_{\text{burst}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)$.
    \item \emph{Block Drops}: Simulating temporary camera occlusions by setting signal confidence to zero for $5{\pm}2$ contiguous frames.
    \item \emph{Random-Walk Drift}: Mimicking gradual user displacement relative to the camera center through a Brownian motion process.
\end{enumerate}
This multi-layered data generation strategy ensures that the TBIE is trained and evaluated on data that is both technically controlled and behaviorally authentic, providing a robust testbed for temporal fusion.

\subsection{Ethical and Practical Rationale for Synthetic Modeling}

The reliance on synthetic behavioral modeling for this study---beyond the fixed identity embeddings derived from VGGFace2---is necessitated by the extreme scarcity of publicly available, ground-truth exam integrity datasets. Real-world proctoring data is often proprietary, and existing benchmarks rarely provide the fine-grained, frame-level anomaly labels required for individual expert model validation. Furthermore, significant privacy considerations regarding the storage and processing of student biometric video data make synthetic generation a responsible alternative for developing baseline defensive systems. This approach allows for the deliberate simulation of complex adversarial behaviors---such as sophisticated identity substitution via gradual drift---while maintaining a controlled, reproducible, and ethically sound experimental framework.

\subsection{Limitations of Synthetic Evaluation}

While the procedural generation of behavioral signals ensures high internal validity and enables the systematic stress-testing of individual experts under extreme conditions, it is important to acknowledge the inherent gap between synthetic modeling and real-world behavior. The AR(1)-based trajectories represent a linearized approximation of human micro-movements, which in practice may exhibit non-linearities and non-stationary noise distributions. Furthermore, the synthetic classes, while behaviorally motivated, do not encompass the full spectrum of anthropogenic cheating strategies. Consequently, the performance metrics reported in this study should be interpreted as a controlled validation of the architectural capabilities, with comprehensive real-world validation on diverse, in-the-wild proctoring datasets remaining a subject for future investigation.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

Let $\mathcal{S} = \{I_1, I_2, \ldots, I_T\}$ denote a sequence of $T$ webcam frames captured during an exam session, and let $I_{\mathrm{ref}}$ denote a single enrollment image captured before the session begins. The system must produce a risk trajectory $\bm{\rho} = (\rho_1, \rho_2, \ldots, \rho_T)$ where each $\rho_t \in [0,1]$ represents the accumulated evidence for an integrity violation at time~$t$. Formally, the integrity of a session is modeled as a latent state evolution within a partially observable framework. Instantaneous visual observations are treated as noisy projections of the underlying behavioral state. The objective is to construct a recursive Bayesian filter that approximates the posterior probability of an ongoing integrity violation based on all evidence gathered up to time $t$:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_t \approx \mathbb{P}(\text{Violation at } t \mid I_{1:t}, I_{\mathrm{ref}})
\end{equation}
As direct calculation of this posterior from raw high-resolution video is computationally prohibitive, the observation space is factorized into six semantically distinct sub-manifolds, such as identity, presence, and gaze dynamics. A recurrent neural architecture is then employed to parameterize the sequential update function.

\noindent The system enforces four core design invariants: (\textbf{I1}) all behavioral interpretation is learned without relying on handcrafted thresholds or heuristic rules; (\textbf{I2}) the reference enrollment embedding remains strictly immutable to prevent identity drift vulnerabilities; (\textbf{I3}) the architecture follows a model-first approach where computer vision components extract measurements for independent behavioral interpretation; and (\textbf{I4}) decision-making is purely temporal, producing a continuous risk trajectory rather than isolated frame-level binary flags.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:methodology}

The proposed architecture comprises seven neural components organized into three stages: \emph{Enrollment}, \emph{Per-Frame Signal Extraction}, and \emph{Probabilistic Risk Fusion}. The complete architecture is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}. Centrally, Fig.~\ref{fig:flow} visualizes the core methodological premise of this work: the systematic accumulation of temporal evidence from multiple individually weak and noisy behavioral signals into a stable, high-confidence risk trajectory that suppresses frame-level sensor artifacts.

\subsection{Stage~1: Enrollment and Identity Anchoring}

The \textbf{Identity Embedding Engine (IDE)} serves as the foundation for identity verification. It utilizes a ResNet-50~\cite{He2016} backbone, which is first pre-trained on ImageNet and subsequently fine-tuned on the \textbf{VGGFace2} dataset~\cite{Cao2018} using a triplet loss objective. This fine-tuning process enables the model to extract highly discriminative 256-dimensional $L_2$-normalized embeddings:
\begin{equation}
    e_0 = \frac{f_\theta(I_{\mathrm{ref}})}{\lVert f_\theta(I_{\mathrm{ref}}) \rVert_2} \in \mathbb{R}^{256}, \quad \lVert e_0 \rVert_2 = 1
    \label{eq:ide}
\end{equation}
where $f_\theta$ denotes the feature extractor with parameters~$\theta$ optimized for facial identity discrimination. By invariant~\textbf{I2}, $e_0$ remains strictly immutable throughout the session, preventing the adversarial drift vulnerabilities often found in systems that adapt their user templates live.

\subsection{Stage~2: Per-Frame Signal Extraction}

For each frame~$I_t$, the IDE extracts a probe embedding $e_t = f_\theta(I_t)/\lVert f_\theta(I_t) \rVert_2$ and computes three derived quantities:
\begin{align}
    S_t &= e_t^\top e_0 & & \text{(cosine similarity)} \label{eq:sim} \\
    \delta_t &= e_t - e_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{256} & & \text{(embedding displacement)} \label{eq:disp} \\
    \mathbf{p}_t &= [c_t, a_t, \psi_t, \theta_t, \phi_t, m_t]^\top & & \text{(presence features)} \label{eq:pres}
\end{align}
where $c_t$ is face detection confidence, $a_t$ is face-to-frame area ratio, $(\psi_t, \theta_t, \phi_t)$ are head pose angles (yaw, pitch, roll), and $m_t$ is optical-flow motion energy.

Five specialized temporal experts process these features:

\subsubsection{Identity Instability Model (IIM)}
An LSTM with hidden dimension~32 processes the similarity sequence to detect abrupt impersonation and flickering substitution:
\begin{equation}
    U_t = \sigma\!\left(W_I \cdot \mathrm{LSTM}(S_t,\, h_{t-1}^{\mathrm{IIM}}) + b_I\right) \in [0,1]
    \label{eq:iim}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Presence and Attentiveness Model (PAM)}
A Bi-LSTM (hidden dimension~64, dropout~$p{=}0.3$) models student presence and engagement from the 6D feature vector~$\mathbf{p}_t$:
\begin{equation}
    P_t = \sigma\!\left(W_P \cdot \mathrm{BiLSTM}_{64}(\mathbf{p}_t) + b_P\right) \in [0,1]
    \label{eq:pam}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Long-Term Drift Detector (LDD)}
A Bi-LSTM (hidden dimension~128) operates on a 257-dimensional input over a 120-frame buffer to detect gradual identity drift:
\begin{equation}
    D_t = \sigma\!\left(W_D \cdot \mathrm{BiLSTM}_{128}([\delta_t \| S_t]_{t-119:t}) + b_D\right)
    \label{eq:ldd}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Gaze Attentiveness Model (GAM)}
A 2-layer Bi-LSTM (hidden dimension~64, dropout~$p{=}0.3$) processes gaze features $\mathbf{g}_t = [\psi_t^g, \theta_t^g, x_t^p, y_t^p, b_t, v_t^g]^\top$ over 120-frame windows:
\begin{equation}
    G_t = \sigma\!\left(W_G \cdot \mathrm{BiLSTM}_{64}^{(2)}(\mathbf{g}_{t-119:t}) + b_G\right)
    \label{eq:gam}
\end{equation}
where $\psi_t^g$, $\theta_t^g$ are gaze yaw/pitch, $(x_t^p, y_t^p)$ are pupil coordinates, $b_t$ is blink ratio, and $v_t^g$ is gaze angular velocity.

\subsubsection{Head-Gaze Dynamics Model (HGDM)}
A 2-layer Bi-LSTM (hidden dimension~64) detects pose-gaze decorrelation from a 7D input including angular velocities:
\begin{equation}
    H_t = \sigma\!\left(W_H \cdot \mathrm{BiLSTM}_{64}^{(2)}(\mathbf{hg}_{t-119:t}) + b_H\right)
    \label{eq:hgdm}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{hg}_t = [\psi_t^h, \theta_t^h, \phi_t^h, \psi_t^g, \theta_t^g, \Delta\theta_t^h, \Delta\theta_t^g]^\top$ and $\Delta\theta_t^{(\cdot)} = \theta_t^{(\cdot)} - \theta_{t-1}^{(\cdot)}$.

\subsection{Stage~3: Probabilistic Risk Fusion}

The \textbf{Risk Fusion Engine (RFE)} is a GRU~\cite{Cho2014} that maintains a hidden state~$h_t$ encoding cumulative session evidence. At each timestep, the six signals are concatenated into a risk vector:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{r}_t = [S_t,\, U_t,\, P_t,\, D_t,\, G_t,\, H_t]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^6
    \label{eq:riskvec}
\end{equation}
The GRU update equations are:
\begin{align}
    z_t &= \sigma(W_z \mathbf{r}_t + U_z h_{t-1} + b_z) \label{eq:gru_z} \\
    r_t &= \sigma(W_r \mathbf{r}_t + U_r h_{t-1} + b_r) \label{eq:gru_r} \\
    \tilde{h}_t &= \tanh(W_h \mathbf{r}_t + U_h (r_t \odot h_{t-1}) + b_h) \label{eq:gru_h} \\
    h_t &= (1 - z_t) \odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde{h}_t \label{eq:gru_update}
\end{align}
To enable uncertainty-aware risk estimation, the engine predicts a Gaussian distribution over the risk logit, parameterized by mean~$\mu_t$ and log-variance~$\log \sigma_t^2$, by projecting the hidden state:
\begin{equation}
    [\mu_t, \log \sigma_t^2]^\top = W_o h_t + b_o 
    \label{eq:uncertainty_proj}
\end{equation}
The continuous risk and uncertainty trajectories are subsequently extracted via element-wise constraints:
\begin{align}
    \rho_t &= \sigma(\mu_t) \in [0, 1] \label{eq:rho} \\
    \sigma_t &= \exp(0.5 \cdot \log \sigma_t^2) > 0 \label{eq:sigma}
\end{align}
trained with session-level binary cross-entropy (BCE). A critical design choice is the utilization of weak session-level supervision applied uniformly across the temporal dimension:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}} = -\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} w_y \bigl[ y \log \rho_t + (1{-}y) \log(1{-}\rho_t) \bigr]
    \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $y \in \{0,1\}$ is the session-level binary label (genuine vs.\ anomalous). To counteract the severe class imbalance inherent to anomaly detection tasks---where genuine events drastically outnumber true integrity violations in real-world distributions---a class balancing weight $w_y$ is introduced, which is inversely proportional to the prior class frequencies. This formulation intentionally avoids the requisite of frame-level strong annotations, which are prohibitively expensive and subject to high inter-rater variance, opting instead to let the GRU discover the temporal salient features corresponding to the global session label.

\subsection{Epistemic and Aleatoric Uncertainty Disentanglement}

The architectural transition from a singular risk logit to the Gaussian parameterization address the duality of uncertainty in behavioral monitoring. \emph{Aleatoric uncertainty} (data noise) arising from poor webcam resolution, transient lighting occlusion, and non-deterministic frame dropping is explicitly codified into the predicted log-variance $\log \sigma_t^2$. This enables the primary risk probability $\rho_t$ to reflect the actual temporal behavioral intent uncorrupted by isolated sensor degradation. Conversely, \emph{epistemic uncertainty} (model ignorance) is minimized through continuous session-level temporal optimization. Consequently, the RFE transcends scalar anomaly scoring, providing a mathematically calibrated credibility interval that allows automated proctoring deployments to safely defer ambiguous boundary cases to human review rather than executing brittle, automated disqualifications.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:inference} summarizes the comprehensive forward-pass inference procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{TBIE Session Inference}
\label{alg:inference}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Enrollment image $I_{\mathrm{ref}}$; frame sequence $\{I_1, \ldots, I_T\}$
\ENSURE Risk trajectory $\bm{\rho} = (\rho_1, \ldots, \rho_T)$
\STATE $e_0 \leftarrow f_\theta(I_{\mathrm{ref}}) / \lVert f_\theta(I_{\mathrm{ref}}) \rVert_2$ \COMMENT{Immutable anchor}
\STATE $h_0 \leftarrow \mathbf{0}$ \COMMENT{Initialize GRU state}
\FOR{$t = 1$ \TO $T$}
    \STATE $e_t \leftarrow f_\theta(I_t) / \lVert f_\theta(I_t) \rVert_2$
    \STATE $S_t \leftarrow e_t^\top e_0$;\quad $\delta_t \leftarrow e_t - e_0$
    \STATE $U_t \leftarrow \mathrm{IIM}(S_t)$ \COMMENT{Identity instability}
    \STATE $P_t \leftarrow \mathrm{PAM}(\mathbf{p}_t)$ \COMMENT{Presence}
    \STATE $D_t \leftarrow \mathrm{LDD}([\delta_t \| S_t])$ \COMMENT{Drift}
    \STATE $G_t \leftarrow \mathrm{GAM}(\mathbf{g}_t)$ \COMMENT{Gaze}
    \STATE $H_t \leftarrow \mathrm{HGDM}(\mathbf{hg}_t)$ \COMMENT{Head-gaze}
    \STATE $\mathbf{r}_t \leftarrow [S_t, U_t, P_t, D_t, G_t, H_t]^\top$
    \STATE $h_t \leftarrow \mathrm{GRU}(\mathbf{r}_t, h_{t-1})$
    \STATE $[\mu_t, \log \sigma_t^2]^\top \leftarrow W_o h_t + b_o$
    \STATE $\rho_t \leftarrow \sigma(\mu_t)$
    \STATE $\sigma_t \leftarrow \exp(0.5 \cdot \log \sigma_t^2)$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\bm{\rho}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The implementation of the TBIE follows a decoupled two-stage training strategy designed to maximize the specialization of each behavioral engine before higher-level fusion. This approach prevents gradient destabilization and ensures each expert achieves high discriminative power within its respective domain.

\subsubsection{Expert Specilaization (Stage 1)}
The six specialized temporal models were trained independently using behavioral signals derived from the synthetic and RVD sources.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{IDE}: Fine-tuned from ResNet-50 using triplet loss ($m=0.2$) on 128,000 alignment-corrected image pairs from VGGFace2.
    \item \textbf{IIM}: A hidden-dimension 32 LSTM optimized with Adam ($LR=5{\times}10^{-4}$) to detect abrupt similarity drops.
    \item \textbf{PAM}: A Bi-LSTM (hidden dimension 64) trained on the 6D feature vector $\mathbf{p}_t$ with dropout $p=0.3$ to suppress environmental noise.
    \item \textbf{LDD}: The primary drift expert, utilizing a Bi-LSTM (hidden dimension 128) over 120-frame buffers to detect embedding manifold displacement.
    \item \textbf{GAM}: A 2-layer Bi-LSTM (hidden dimension 64) trained on the 6D gaze vector $\mathbf{g}_t$ to classify gaze-based anomalies.
    \item \textbf{HGDM}: A 2-layer Bi-LSTM (hidden dimension 64) specifically optimized to detect pose-gaze decorrelation through angular velocity signals.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Temporal Risk Fusion (Stage 2)}
Once the experts achieved domain-specific convergence, their frozen outputs were passed to the Risk Fusion Engine (RFE). The GRU-based fusion layer was optimized for 20 epochs using the session-level loss function defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:loss}, employing an AdamW optimizer with a linear learning rate warm-up and cosine annealing. This stage ensures that the global integrity decision is informed by the long-term interaction between heterogeneous behavioral dimensions.
\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Given the binary classification framing at the session level, the evaluation is formalized using four core metrics. The continuous risk output is assessed via the ROC-AUC and the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (PR-AUC). Due to the prevalence of the negative (genuine) class in operational proctoring, PR-AUC provides a more conservative and informative assessment of false-positive resilience.

Predictive calibration is also evaluated using the Brier Score and Expected Calibration Error (ECE). The Brier Score assesses the mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes, while the ECE partitions predictions into equally spaced bins to compute the weighted average of the absolute difference between accuracy and confidence per bin.

\section{Temporal Interpretability}
\label{sec:xai}

Deployment in high-stakes educational settings requires interpretable risk assessments. Temporal attribution is implemented via gradient-based local sensitivity analysis~\cite{Sundararajan2017}.

For each frame~$t$ and signal~$i \in \{S, U, P, D, G, H\}$, the contribution to $\rho_t$ is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{t,i} = \left| \frac{\partial \rho_t}{\partial r_{t,i}} \right|, \qquad
    \hat{\alpha}_{t,i} = \frac{\alpha_{t,i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{6} \alpha_{t,j}}
    \label{eq:attribution}
\end{equation}
producing a $T \times 6$ attribution matrix. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:xai}, the RFE assigns high importance to $D_t$ and $U_t$ during substitution attacks while suppressing gaze and presence channels, enabling human proctors to validate which behavioral dimension triggered the risk elevation.

\label{fig:xai}
\end{figure}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The low per-frame accuracy of individual components is a deliberate methodological choice. If a near-perfect face matcher were utilized, temporal modeling would be redundant. By demonstrating that the fusion engine achieves high accuracy from weak inputs, the core thesis is validated: \emph{temporal accumulation captures behavioral intent that instantaneous observations cannot resolve}. This represents a key practical value proposition—reliable monitoring with imperfect signals under uncontrolled conditions.

\subsection{Primacy of Long-Horizon Drift Detection}

LDD ablation produces a significantly larger impact on accuracy than identity embedding ablation. This asymmetry is architecturally principled: LDD is the only component accumulating an extended trajectory of displacement, making it uniquely sensitive to sophisticated attacks such as gradual substitution. Short-window models generally fail to capture this pattern.

\subsection{Value of Temporal Ordering}

The improvement of the recurrent architecture over order-agnostic models is statistically significant, demonstrating that \emph{when} evidence appears carries critical diagnostic information. The temporal profile of anomaly onset provides information that order-invariant architectures typically discard.

\subsection{Cross-Modal Consistency Checking}

The head-gaze dynamics model achieves high individual performance by detecting pose-gaze decorrelation—an anomaly dimension orthogonal to identity-based signals. Phone usage, for example, often results in a stable head position combined with deviated gaze, a pattern that is detectable through their sustained decorrelation.

\subsection{Uncertainty-Aware False Positive Mitigation}

A persistent criticism of automated proctoring is the generation of unjustified flags due to environmental noise (e.g., poor lighting, partial occlusion). The integration of continuous uncertainty estimation ($\sigma_t$) fundamentally addresses this by decoupling the risk magnitude ($\rho_t$) from prediction confidence. Rather than acting on a raw scalar, institutions can implement dual-threshold policies: high-risk/low-uncertainty events can be flagged for immediate review, while high-risk/high-uncertainty events (often caused by sensor degradation) can be deferred or discarded, explicitly protecting examinees from algorithmic brittleness.

\subsection{Deployment Considerations}

The modular architecture supports incremental deployment: institutions can begin with identity and presence signals, adding gaze and dynamics modules as infrastructure permits. The ResNet-50 backbone is the latency bottleneck and can be substituted with lightweight alternatives~\cite{Howard2019, Tan2019}. Furthermore, the system is highly amenable to edge deployment; by leveraging ONNX or TensorFlow.js, the TBIE can be integrated directly into client-side browser environments, eliminating the need for centralized video stream processing and significantly enhancing user privacy through local-only inference.

\section{Ethical Considerations and Limitations}
\label{sec:ethics}

\subsection{Ethical Design}

The system output consists of continuous risk probabilities, which is intended to prevent automated disqualification. Final decisions remain with human proctors who are provided with temporal attribution data. The one-shot enrollment constraint also helps minimize data retention, as no persistent behavioral profile is stored.

\subsection{Demographic Bias}

Facial recognition backbones are known to carry demographic biases~\cite{Buolamwini2018}. The developed system operates on \emph{relative} similarity rather than absolute classification, which partially mitigates this concern. However, if a backbone produces systematically lower similarity for certain demographics, disproportionate false flagging could still occur. Disaggregated evaluation on diverse populations is therefore necessary before practical deployment.

\subsection{Limitations}

Continuing research and real-world validation are necessary to confirm how well synthetic signal distributions mirror the stochastic conditions of actual webcam use. Furthermore, the visual backbone represents a latency bottleneck that might exceed tight real-time budgets on pure CPU constraints; lightweight alternatives should be considered for such production environments. Additionally, the temporal buffer required for drift detection introduces a short initial window where brief impersonations might evade fusion. Finally, adapting the system for academic exams of arbitrary length or ensuring robust performance against sophisticated adversarial attacks remains an area for ongoing refinement.

%                     X. CONCLUSION
% ===================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study has presented TBIE, a seven-component neural architecture designed to replace traditional threshold-based proctoring with continuous probabilistic behavioral inference. The proposed system achieves a high session-level ROC-AUC by effectively fusing multiple individually weak behavioral signals, demonstrating that the accumulation of temporal evidence fundamentally outperforms instantaneous event detection. Key findings from this research indicate that long-horizon drift detection is a critical signal for identifying sophisticated misconduct, while cross-modal head-gaze consistency is essential for detecting pose-gaze decorrelation. Furthermore, the integration of continuous uncertainty estimation allows the framework to natively compute prediction confidence, thereby preventing algorithmically brittle false flags and protecting examinees from unjustified accusations. 

The broader implications of this research extend beyond technical performance; by transitioning from binary ``cheating detection'' to continuous ``integrity monitoring,'' the TBIE framework supports a more equitable and transparent educational environment. The inclusion of temporal attribution heatmaps empowers human proctors with the granular evidence necessary to make informed, data-driven decisions, reducing the likelihood of bias and ensuring that student rights are protected. Future research directions include the development of ordinal risk labels to distinguish between minor distractions and severe integrity breaches, as well as the implementation of lightweight visual backbones for enhanced real-time performance on standard processing units. Additionally, validating the framework on larger and more diverse real-world datasets remains an essential objective to ensure its broad applicability and fairness across different demographic populations, particularly in bridging the gap between controlled experimentation and unconstrained deployment.
